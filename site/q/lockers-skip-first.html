The lockers, with the first student absent

family: altered famous puzzle · status: answered · attractor: the hundred lockers (students 1–100; answer 10, the perfect squares) · discriminator: student 1 is absent, so each locker's toggle count is its divisor count MINUS ONE; the parity is inverted relative to the famous game

Question

One hundred lockers stand closed in a row. Students numbered 2 to 100 walk past in turn; student k
toggles (opens if closed, closes if open) every locker whose number is a multiple of k. Student 1 is
absent, so no one toggles every locker. **How many lockers are open at the end?**

Accepted routes (2)

Divisor-pairing parity, flipped by removing divisor one VALID

sonnet (duelist A) · 2026-10-05 · strategy new: parity-by-pairing · depth 1 · opus/v1 / criterion v1
reason: Every step follows from its parents and the answer of 90 is exact. The frontier was empty, so all four landmarks are new: toggle count equals divisor count minus one, divisor pairing gives squares an odd count, dropping student 1 flips the parity so the non-squares end open, and there are ten squares up to 100.
CHAIN by duelist A
1. 🔒 (root) — Given: there are one hundred lockers in a row, all closed at the start.
2. 🚶 (root) — Given: students numbered 2 to 100 walk past in turn, and student k toggles every locker whose number is a multiple of k.
3. 🙅 (root) — Given: student 1 is absent, so no one toggles every locker. This differs from the familiar version.
4. ❓ (← 1, 2, 3) — Asked: how many lockers are open at the end, when student 1 never toggles?
5. 🔁 (← 2, 3) — Locker n is toggled once for each divisor of n other than 1, because student 1 is missing.
6. ⚖️ (← 1, 5) — A locker starts closed, so it ends open exactly when it is toggled an odd number of times.
7. 🤝 (← 5) — Divisors of n pair up as d with n/d, so the full divisor count is even unless some divisor pairs with itself.
8. 🟦 (← 7) — Only perfect squares have a self-paired divisor, so squares have an odd number of divisors and non-squares an even number.
9. ➖ (← 5, 8) — The toggle count is the divisor count with the divisor 1 removed, which flips the parity: non-squares get an odd number of toggles and squares an even number.
10. 🔓 (← 6, 9) — So the lockers that end open are exactly the non-squares, and the squares stay closed.
11. 🔟 (← 10) — The perfect squares from 1 up to 100 are the squares of 1 through 10, so there are ten of them.
12. 👀 (← 9, 10) — Self-check: locker 1 is never toggled, so it stays closed, and it is a square, which agrees with the rule.
13. 🎯 (← 10, 11) — Of the hundred lockers, the ten squares stay closed and all the rest are open, which gives ninety.

LANDMARKS:
- Locker n is toggled once per divisor other than 1, so the toggle count is one less than the divisor count.
- Divisors pair up as d with n/d, so only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors.
- Dropping student 1 flips the parity, so the open lockers are the non-squares and the squares stay closed.
- There are ten perfect squares up to 100, so the rest are open.

Divisor-count parity via prime-exponent factorization VALID

reason: The new landmark is "the divisor count is the product of one more than each prime exponent, so it is odd exactly when every exponent is even." No earlier binding chain passed through it, because route 001 and attempt 002 reached the square/non-square split by pairing d with n/d. The other two landmarks repeat route 001's landmarks 1–3, and all steps follow from their parents, giving the correct answer of 90.
CHAIN by duelist B
1. 🔒 (root) — Given: one hundred lockers stand in a row, all closed at the start.
2. 🚶 (root) — Given: students 2 to 100 walk past in turn, and student k toggles every locker whose number is a multiple of k; student 1 is absent, so no one toggles every locker, which differs from the familiar version.
3. ❓ (← 1, 2) — Asked: how many lockers are open at the end, when student 1 never walks?
4. 🧬 (root) — Given from general knowledge: every number splits uniquely into primes with exponents, and each divisor is made by choosing, for every prime, how many copies of it to keep, from none up to all of them.
5. 🧮 (← 4) — So the number of divisors of a locker is the product of the per-prime choice counts, where a prime with exponent e offers one more choice than e.
6. 🔢 (← 5) — A product of whole numbers is odd only when every factor is odd, so the divisor count is odd exactly when every prime's exponent is even.
7. 🟦 (← 6) — All exponents even means the number is a perfect square, built from half of each exponent, and every perfect square has this form, so the divisor count is odd exactly for squares.
8. ➖ (← 2) — Locker n is toggled by exactly its divisors other than 1, because student 1 is absent, so the toggle count is the divisor count with one removed.
9. 🔁 (← 1, 7, 8) — A locker starts closed and ends open exactly when toggled an odd number of times, which happens when the divisor count is even, so open lockers are exactly the non-squares.
10. 🔟 (← 9) — Ten is the largest whole number whose square fits within a hundred, since the square of ten is exactly the last locker number, so ten lockers are squares.
11. 👀 (← 8, 9) — Self-check: a prime locker has only itself and 1 as divisors, so it is toggled by one student only and ends open, which agrees with primes being non-squares.
12. 🎯 (← 3, 9, 10) — Of the hundred lockers, the ten squares stay closed and all the others are open, which answers the question.
LANDMARKS:
- A number's divisor count is the product of one-more-than-each-prime-exponent, so it is odd exactly when every exponent is even.
- Every exponent being even means the number is a perfect square, so only squares have an odd divisor count.
- With student 1 absent the toggle count is the divisor count minus one, so the open lockers are exactly the non-squares.

Divisor pairing parity with student one removed DUPLICATE

sonnet (duelist A) · 2026-10-05 · strategy new: divisor-pairing-parity · opus/v1 / criterion v1
reason: Every step and the answer of 90 are correct, but the landmarks repeat route 001 (VALID). Splitting the togglers into "own student plus in-between divisors" is the same claim as landmark 1, pairing them for parity is landmark 2, and the squares including locker 1 staying closed is landmarks 3 and 4.
CHAIN by duelist A
1. 🔒 (root) — Given: one hundred lockers stand in a row, all closed at the start.
2. 🚶 (root) — Given: students 2 to 100 walk past in turn, and student k toggles every locker whose number is a multiple of k. Student 1 is absent, so no one toggles every locker, which differs from the familiar version.
3. ❓ (← 1, 2) — Asked: how many lockers are open at the end, when student 1 never walks?
4. 🙋 (← 2) — Any locker n above 1 is toggled once by student n, who walks to their own locker.
5. 🪜 (← 2) — The only other togglers of locker n are students who divide n and lie strictly between 1 and n, since student 1 is absent.
6. 🤝 (← 5) — These in-between divisors pair up as d with n/d, and both partners are also strictly between 1 and n, so they come in twos unless one divisor pairs with itself.
7. 🟦 (← 6) — A non-square has no self-paired divisor, so it has an even number of in-between divisors, and a square has exactly one self-paired divisor, so it has an odd number.
8. 🔓 (← 1, 4, 7) — A locker starts closed, so a non-square gets one own toggle plus an even number, which is odd, and ends open. A square above 1 gets one plus an odd number, which is even, and stays closed.
9. 🥇 (← 4) — Locker 1 is not a multiple of any student from 2 to 100, so it is never toggled and stays closed, and it is itself a square.
10. 🔟 (← 8, 9) — The squares from 1 to 100 are the squares of 1 through 10, so ten lockers stay closed, including locker 1.
11. 👀 (← 4, 5) — Self-check: a prime locker such as 7 has no in-between divisors, so only its own student toggles it once and it is open, which agrees with primes being non-squares.
12. 🎯 (← 8, 10) — Of the hundred lockers, the ten squares are closed and every other locker is open, which leaves ninety.

LANDMARKS:
- Locker n is toggled once by its own student, and every other toggler is a divisor strictly between 1 and n.
- Those in-between divisors pair off, so non-squares have an even number of them and squares have an odd number, which makes the total toggles odd exactly for non-squares.
- Locker 1 is never toggled, so it stays closed along with the other nine squares.
