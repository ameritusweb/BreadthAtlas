A wire cube seen along its diagonal

family: spatial reconstruction · status: answered · attractor: none

Question

A wire-frame cube (edges only) is viewed in orthographic projection exactly along a space diagonal —
from one corner, through the centre, to the opposite corner. **What shape is the outline of the figure,
and how many of the twelve edges lie on that outline?**

Accepted routes (2)

Three edge directions bound the convex outline's side count VALID

sonnet (duelist B) · 2026-10-05 · strategy new: direction-counting · depth 1 · opus/v1 / criterion v1
reason: The chain is sound: it counts the edge directions and uses the shape's symmetry to bound and then fix the number of outline sides, giving the correct answer of a regular hexagon with 6 edges. Two landmarks are new: "a convex outline using only three directions has at most six sides, at most two per direction" and "each direction's four edges split two on the outline and two inside." No earlier chain passed through either claim.
CHAIN by duelist B
1. 🧊 (root) — Given: a cube has twelve edges, four parallel to each of three mutually perpendicular directions.
2. 👁️ (root) — Given: the view is orthographic, along a space diagonal from one corner through the centre to the opposite corner, and all edges are drawn as a wire frame.
3. ❓ (← 1, 2) — Asked: what shape is the outline of this picture, and how many of the twelve edges lie on the outline? This is not the familiar face-on square view.
4. 📐 (← 1, 2) — The diagonal is tilted equally to all three edge directions, so each direction is foreshortened by the same amount.
5. 🔱 (← 4) — With equal tilt, the three directions show up as lines evenly spread around the picture, 60° apart as lines.
6. 📏 (← 4) — Parallel edges are equal, and every direction is foreshortened equally, so all twelve edges appear the same length.
7. 🧩 (← 1, 5) — The whole picture is therefore built from segments in only three line-directions, four segments in each.
8. 🧱 (← 6, 7) — The outline is a closed convex shape made of equal-length segments drawn from those three directions.
9. 🔒 (← 5, 8) — A convex outline can use each direction for at most two sides, one on each opposite side, so it has at most six sides.
10. 🔄 (← 5, 9) — The cube is centrally symmetric, so the outline is too, and a triangle is ruled out. The outline also has a third-turn symmetry about the diagonal, which rules out a parallelogram. That leaves six sides, two per direction.
11. ⬡ (← 6, 9, 10) — Six equal sides with that symmetry make the outline a regular hexagon.
12. 🧮 (← 1, 7, 11) — Each direction has four edges, and two of them are hexagon sides. That gives six outline edges, and the other two per direction lie inside, as the six spokes.
13. 👀 (← 12) — Self-check: six outline edges plus six inner spokes make twelve, so none is missing or counted twice.
14. 🏁 (← 3, 11, 12) — The outline is a regular hexagon, and six edges lie on it.

LANDMARKS:
- The diagonal is tilted equally to all three edge directions, so all twelve edges appear the same length, in three line-directions 60° apart.
- A convex outline using only three directions has at most six sides, with at most two sides per direction.
- Central symmetry and third-turn symmetry together force exactly six sides, so the outline is a regular hexagon.
- Each direction's four edges split two on the outline and two inside, giving six on the outline.

ANSWER: A regular hexagon, with 6 of the 12 edges on the outline (the other 6 appear as spokes inside it).

Zigzag crown of edges forms the hull outline VALID

reason: The steps hold up: the six edges that avoid both diagonal ends do form a closed zigzag. Two cube symmetries make that loop appear as a regular hexagon, and its edges are the outline. The new landmarks are the closed loop alternating near and far around the line of sight (9) and the outline as the convex hull fixed by that loop alone (10); neither appears in attempts 001–002, which came before it.

CHAIN by duelist A
1. 🧊 (root) — Given: a cube has 8 corners and 12 equal edges, and each edge joins two corners.
2. 👁️ (root) — Given: the view is orthographic, looking exactly along a space diagonal, from one corner through the centre to the opposite corner.
3. ❓ (← 1, 2) — Asked: what shape is the outline of the wire cube seen this way, and how many of its twelve edges lie on that outline? Only edges are drawn, with no faces.
4. 🔄 (← 1) — The six corners that are not diagonal ends are three neighbours of the near end and three neighbours of the far end. Each near neighbour is joined by an edge to two far neighbours. So six edges form one closed zigzag loop through those six corners and avoid both end corners.
5. ↕️ (← 4) — Going round the loop, the corners alternate between the near side and the far side of the cube. The loop is a crown wrapped around the waist of the cube, circling the line of sight.
6. 🔺 (root) — Given: the outline of a see-through solid in orthographic view is the convex hull of its projected corners.
7. 🔁 (← 4, 5) — A third-turn about the diagonal carries the loop onto itself, and so does the half-turn flip that swaps near and far. So the six loop corners appear equally spaced and equally distant around the picture's centre.
8. 🎯 (← 4, 6) — The two end corners lie on the line of sight, so they project to the centre, inside the ring of the six loop corners. The hull is therefore fixed by the six loop corners alone.
9. ⬡ (← 6, 7, 8) — The convex hull of six equally spaced, equidistant points is a regular hexagon. So the outline is a regular hexagon.
10. 🔢 (← 4, 9) — Neighbouring hexagon corners are neighbours in the loop, so each hexagon side is a loop edge. Six edges lie on the outline.
11. 🧮 (← 4, 10) — The other six edges each touch an end corner, which projects to the centre. They run from the centre to the rim and lie inside the outline.
12. 👀 (← 10, 11) — Self-check: six edges on the rim plus six edges inside make twelve, so every edge is counted once.

LANDMARKS:
- The six edges avoiding both diagonal ends form one closed zigzag loop, a crown of corners alternating near and far, that circles the line of sight.
- The outline is the convex hull of the projected corners, and the end corners, which project to the centre, add nothing to it, so the loop alone makes the outline.
- Rotation and flip symmetry make the loop's six corners appear regular, so the hull is a regular hexagon whose sides are the loop edges.
ANSWER: A regular hexagon, with 6 of the 12 edges on the outline (the other 6 lie inside it).

4. 🔁 (← 1) — Turning the cube a third of a full turn about a space diagonal maps the cube onto itself, so the three edges at an end corner are interchangeable.
5. 🎯 (← 2) — The two diagonal end corners lie on the line of sight, so each projects to the centre of the picture.
6. ✳️ (← 4, 5) — By that symmetry, the three edges from the near corner appear as three equal spokes from the centre, evenly spread around it.
7. 🪞 (← 1, 6) — The cube is also symmetric under swapping opposite corners, so the far corner's three edges appear as three more equal spokes. They point opposite to the near spokes and fall between them.
8. 🔆 (← 6, 7) — Six equal spokes at even spacing end in six distinct points around the centre, which are the other six corners.
9. 🧵 (← 1, 8) — The six edges that touch neither end corner each join two neighbouring ring points, because each such edge joins a near-side corner to a far-side corner.
10. ⬡ (← 8, 9) — Joining the six ring points in order with six equal sides gives a closed figure with equal sides and equal angles, which is a regular hexagon.
11. 🔍 (← 7, 10) — All six spokes run from the centre to the ring, so they lie inside the hexagon and do not form part of its boundary.
12. 👀 (← 9, 11) — Self-check: the rim edges are the ones avoiding both end corners, and the spokes are the ones touching an end corner. These two groups share no edge and together cover every edge of the cube.
13. ✅ (← 10, 11, 12) — The outline is a regular hexagon formed by exactly six cube edges, and the other six edges are interior spokes.

- A third-turn symmetry about the diagonal makes the three edges at an end corner look identical, evenly spread, and equally foreshortened.
- Opposite-corner symmetry means the far corner's three edges appear as spokes opposite the near ones, falling between them.
- The edges touching neither diagonal end corner are exactly the rim edges, and those touching an end corner are the interior spokes.

ANSWER: A regular hexagon, with six of the twelve edges on the outline.
