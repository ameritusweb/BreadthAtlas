The height of a building, with a barometer

family: open problem, method design · status: open · attractor: the pressure difference between ground and roof (the "intended" answer of the famous story)

Question

You have a barometer and must determine the height of a tall building. **Describe a method and the
principle it relies on.** Each route must rest on a different principle; the barometer must play a
role in the method.

Barometric pressure difference, roof versus ground, converted to height VALID

sonnet (blind player 2) · 2026-10-05 · strategy new: pressure-altimetry · depth 1 · opus/v2 / criterion v2
reason: No binding chains exist yet, so all three landmarks are new: the roof reading is lower than the ground reading; over a building-sized climb the pressure drop is roughly proportional to height; and a known rate of pressure fall per unit height turns the drop into a height. Every step follows from the parents it names, the physics is correct (near the ground the fall is roughly 1 hPa per 8 m and close to linear), and nothing is simulated.
CHAIN by blind player 2
1. 🌡️ (root) — The barometer is a pressure gauge that reads the local air pressure.
2. 🏢 (root) — The building is tall, and both its ground level and its roof can be reached.
3. ❓ (← 1, 2) — Asked: describe a method, and the principle it relies on, that uses the barometer to find the building's height. The barometer must play a role in the method.
4. 🌬️ (root) — Air pressure falls with height, because less air lies above you the higher you go.
5. ⬇️ (← 1, 2, 3) — Read the barometer at ground level.
6. ⬆️ (← 1, 2, 3) — Carry the barometer up to the roof and read it again.
7. 📉 (← 4, 5, 6) — The roof reading comes out lower than the ground reading, as the principle predicts.
8. ⚖️ (← 7) — The difference between the two readings is the pressure drop over the building. Compared with the whole atmospheric pressure, this drop is small.
9. 🪜 (← 4, 8) — Over so small a climb, equal steps of height give nearly equal pressure drops. So the drop is proportional to the height climbed.
10. 🧭 (← 9) — Standard-atmosphere tables give the known rate of pressure fall per unit of height near the ground.
11. 🏁 (← 8, 9, 10) — The measured drop, converted at that known rate, gives the building's height.

LANDMARKS:
- Air pressure is lower at the roof than at the ground, so the barometer reads less there.
- Over a building-sized climb, the pressure drop is roughly proportional to the height climbed.
- A known pressure-per-height rate converts the measured drop into a height.

ANSWER: Read the barometer at the ground and again at the roof. The pressure drop, converted at the known rate at which pressure falls with height, gives the building's height. The principle is that pressure falls with height.

Drop barometer from roof and time its fall VALID

sonnet (blind player 3) · 2026-10-05 · strategy new: free-fall-timing · depth 2 · opus/v2 / criterion v2
reason: This chain uses free fall, a principle that is independent of route 001's pressure gradient. Its landmarks are new: air drag is negligible for a dense, compact barometer, height grows as the square of the fall time, and the timed drop plus gravity gives the roof height. Step 8's "slowed very little" is loose for the tallest buildings but is not wrong by a category. No step tracks state step by step.
CHAIN by blind player 3
1. 🌡️ (root) — The barometer is a heavy, compact object that I can carry up and drop.
2. 🏢 (root) — The building is tall, and I can reach its roof by the stairs.
3. ⏱️ (root) — Time can be measured.
4. ❓ (← 1, 2, 3) — Asked: how do I find the building's height using the barometer, and what principle does the method rest on? Here the route must not use the barometer as a pressure gauge.
5. 🪂 (← 4, 1) — The principle is free fall: an object released from rest falls under gravity, so the height fallen is fixed by the fall time.
6. 🧗 (← 2, 5) — I climb to the roof and hold the barometer out over a cleared patch of ground below the edge.
7. 🎬 (← 3, 6) — I release the barometer and record the drop on video, so the time from release to impact can be read off frame by frame.
8. 🪶 (← 1, 5) — A dense, compact barometer is slowed very little by air over a building's height, so its fall is close to ideal free fall.
9. 📈 (← 5, 8) — In ideal free fall a longer fall means a greater height, and the height grows faster than the time, as the square of the time.
10. 🏁 (← 7, 9) — The measured fall time, together with the known strength of gravity, gives the height. This is the building's height, since the drop started at the roof and ended at the ground.
11. 👀 (← 10) — Self-check: repeated drops should give nearly the same time, and a taller building should always give a longer time. This matches the principle.

LANDMARKS:
- A heavy barometer dropped from the roof falls almost freely, because air drag is small for a dense compact object over a building's height.
- The fall time from rest fixes the height, which grows as the square of the time, so a longer drop means a taller building.
- Timing the drop from release to impact, together with the known strength of gravity, gives the roof height.

ANSWER: Drop the barometer from the roof, time its fall, and use free fall under gravity to turn that time into the building's height.

8. ⚖️ (← 5, 7) — Compare the two readings: the roof reading is lower than the ground reading, and the gap between them reflects the height climbed.
9. 🌬️ (← 4, 8) — Because pressure falls steadily with height near the ground, a bigger gap means a taller building, and the gap converts to height using the known rate of fall of pressure with altitude.
10. 🏢 (← 9) — The converted gap gives the building's height; a tall building gives a clearly measurable gap, while a short one would give a gap too small to read well.
11. 👀 (← 4, 8) — Self-check: the method needs the pressure to be steady between the two readings and the instrument to be sensitive enough, so read both quickly, one soon after the other, on a calm day.

- The difference between the ground and roof pressure readings, converted by the known rate of pressure fall with altitude, gives the height.

ANSWER: Read the barometer at the foot of the building and again on the roof; the pressure difference, converted using the known rate at which air pressure falls with height, gives the building's height.
