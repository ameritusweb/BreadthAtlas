1. 🏊 (root) — Given: an Olympic pool holds a few million litres.
2. 🚰 (root) — Given: an ordinary kitchen tap running flat out delivers about ten litres a minute.
3. 🥤 (root) — Given: a person normally drinks about two litres a day.
4. 🧓 (root) — Given: a long lifetime is about eighty years, which is tens of thousands of days.
5. 🌙 (← 1, 2) — A kitchen tap left running day and night would need roughly half a year to fill the pool. That is many months with no pause.
6. 🗓️ (← 3, 4) — A lifetime of drinking comes to some tens of thousands of litres, a small fraction of the pool.
7. 💧 (← 2, 6) — The same tap would deliver that lifetime total in only a few days, since a few days of running tap carries tens of thousands of litres.
8. ⚖️ (← 5, 7) — Half a year of running tap set against a few days of running tap means the pool is dozens of times bigger than a lifetime of drinking.
9. 🏋️ (← 3) — A heavy drinker might take a few times the usual intake, which stretches the few days to a week or two at most.
10. 👀 (← 5, 8, 9) — Self-check: a week or two of tap against half a year of tap still leaves a gap in the tens.
11. 🚫 (← 8, 10) — So no single person could drink a pool's worth of water in one lifetime.
12. 🔢 (← 5, 7, 11) — The pool is bigger by a factor in the tens, about forty.

LANDMARKS:
- A kitchen tap running continuously fills the pool in about half a year.
- The same tap supplies a person's whole lifetime of drinking in only a few days.
- The comparison is tap-time (months versus days), not container units, daily rate, or household use.

ANSWER: No; the pool is bigger by roughly 40 times.
