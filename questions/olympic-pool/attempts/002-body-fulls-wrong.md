1. 🏊 (root) — Given: an Olympic pool holds a few million litres.
2. 🧍 (root) — Given: the water inside an adult body is a few tens of litres, about the volume of a large cooler or a small barrel.
3. 🥤 (root) — Given: a person drinks roughly a couple of litres a day.
4. 🧓 (root) — Given: a long lifetime is about eighty years, which is tens of thousands of days.
5. 🔄 (← 2, 3) — Each day's drinking replaces only a small fraction of the body's water, so the body's whole water store is renewed every couple of weeks.
6. ♾️ (← 4, 5) — Over a lifetime the body's water is renewed over a thousand times, so the lifetime total is a thousand-odd body-fulls.
7. 🧊 (← 1, 2) — The pool is far more than that: it holds tens of thousands of body-fulls of water.
8. 👥 (← 6, 7) — Tens of thousands of body-fulls set against a thousand-odd means the pool is many tens of times the lifetime total. It would cover the lifelong drinking of a small town's worth of people, not one person.
9. 🏋️ (← 3) — A heavy drinker might take several times the normal intake, but that raises the lifetime count only by a small factor.
10. 👀 (← 6, 7, 9) — Self-check: even with the heavy drinker's lifetime count of a few thousand body-fulls, the pool is still many times larger.
11. 🚫 (← 8, 10) — So no one person could drink a pool's worth of water in a lifetime.
12. 🔢 (← 6, 7, 11) — The pool is bigger by a factor in the tens, about forty.

LANDMARKS:
- A body holds only a few tens of litres of water, so that body-full is the yardstick.
- A lifetime of drinking turns the body's water over a thousand-odd times.
- The pool is tens of thousands of body-fulls.
- The pool would cover the lifelong drinking of a small town's worth of people, not one person.

ANSWER: No; the pool is bigger by roughly 40 times.
