# Question

A ping-pong ball sits at the bottom of a narrow steel pipe that is fixed upright to a concrete floor.
The pipe is just wider than the ball and about as tall as your forearm. You are in an ordinary office.
**Describe a way to get the ball out without damaging the pipe or the ball, and the principle it
relies on.** Each route must rest on a different principle.

# Glossary

| emoji | given |
|---|---|
| 🏓 | the ball: light, hollow, floats, slightly compressible, smooth |
| 🧪 | the pipe: narrow, rigid, open at the top, closed by the floor |
| 🏢 | an office: water, tape, paper, pencils, a vacuum cleaner, straws, chewing gum, string |

| emoji | principle |
|---|---|
| 🌊 | buoyancy: fill the pipe, the ball floats up |
| 🌀 | suction: lower pressure above the ball |
| 🧲 | adhesion: something sticky on a stick |
| 🪝 | mechanical grip: a loop or claw that fits the gap |
| 💨 | airflow: a jet past the ball lifts it (Bernoulli / entrainment) |
| 🔥 | thermal: warm air inside the ball expands — only if the ball seals the pipe |
| 🪤 | wedge: friction jaws, two pencils or a folded card |

# Reference answer (referee only)

Open problem. Judge soundness and novelty. Physically weak claims to rule WRONG if load-bearing:
blowing into the pipe lifts the ball (air escapes around it; no seal); a magnet (celluloid is not
magnetic); heating the pipe to expand it enough to matter; static electricity lifting the ball the full
height. Sound routes include buoyancy, a vacuum cleaner or strong suction on a straw, adhesive on a
pencil (gum, tape rolled sticky-side out), a wire or paper-clip hook under the ball if any gap exists, a
tapered paper cone pressed over the ball as friction jaws.

# Why it is in the atlas

A physical open problem with one dominant attractor (water) and several genuinely different principles
behind it; the referee must know enough physics to reject the plausible-sounding ones.
